Reading & Critique

Insights about the data/visualization:

  • The late December 2021 / January 2022 surge was dramatically larger than previous waves, marking the highest case counts of the pandemic in this visualization
  • Cases declined to a relative low in late spring/early summer 2021 before rising again during the mid-2021 summer wave
  • The number of COVID cases each winter is larger than the previous years

Design Critique:

While cool-looking, this spiral design is a bit misleading in my opinion. First, it appears to imply that there were no new COVID-19 cases in the United States until March 2020, since the spiral begins to expand meaningfully only around that point. In reality, the first confirmed U.S. case was reported in January 2020, even though the WHO declared COVID-19 a pandemic in March 2020. By visually anchoring the early part of 2020 near zero and delaying noticeable expansion until March, the chart risks reinforcing the narrative that COVID suddenly came out of nowhere, rather than gradually emerging and spreading in the preceding weeks. The center line is also confusing. The shaded red band represents new case counts, yet the thin black line running through it resembles a traditional "average" reference line, where values above and below signal deviation from a baseline. However, in this visualization it isn't clear what the black line represents. Is it the 7-day average? Additionally, the spiral layout distorts time perception. Months further from the center occupy a larger circumference, so October 2021 visually appears longer than October 2020, even though both contain the same number of days.

More broadly, the radial format amplifies perceived differences in magnitude because values plotted farther from the center occupy more visual area, making later surges feel more dramatic than their raw case counts alone would suggest. The chart also makes precise comparison difficult: viewers must mentally translate radial distance into case counts, and overlapping yearly spirals increase cognitive load. While the design effectively conveys the emotional intensity of the pandemic’s waves, it prioritizes visual impact over analytical clarity. A conventional line chart or small multiples layout would allow for clearer comparisons across years without geometric distortion, making trends easier to interpret and reducing the risk of misleading impressions.

Final Visualization Design

Final Writeup

My goal with the final design was to make the magnitude and timing of COVID-19 surges easy to understand while still preserving the underlying day-to-day variability that shaped public perception and policy. To do that, I combined daily bars (raw reports) with a weekly moving-average line (signal). The bars communicate the volatility and reporting artifacts inherent in daily case data, while the smoothed line makes the broader “wave” structure legible without requiring the viewer to mentally average noisy fluctuations. I also added event annotations (first confirmed case, shutdowns, vaccine rollout milestones, Omicron detection/dominance) to connect peaks and declines to real-world context, helping the chart move beyond “what happened” toward “what might have contributed,” without claiming strict causality.

This redesign directly addresses my original critique of the spiral chart by eliminating the misleading implications and distortions created by radial layout: the timeline is continuous and proportional (no month-length distortion), the trend line is explicitly defined (weekly average rather than an ambiguous center line), and the early-pandemic period is visible, reducing the impression that cases “begin” only at the pandemic declaration. That said, I encountered real tradeoffs. The largest tension was between faithfully representing extreme peaks (especially Omicron) and keeping earlier waves readable. Another tradeoff was between clarity and context: annotations improve interpretability, but too many labels risk cluttering the visual and implying causal relationships more strongly than the data alone supports. What didn’t work as anticipated was how much the raw daily bars still dominate attention during high-variance periods; even with smoothing, the chart can feel busy. If I iterated further, I would likely lighten the bars, reduce gridline prominence, and consider a second panel or zoomed inset to support comparisons among smaller waves without sacrificing the integrity of the full-scale view.
